Soft Robotics for an Adaptive Space Exercise Harnessing System

Astronauts spend upwards of 10 hours exercising each week and still experience significant muscle atrophy and bone density loss. This project focused on developing a soft robotic harness system that can adaptively control loading using sensor feedback and machine learning, with the goal of improving exercise effectiveness in microgravity. As the only electrical team member, I designed and integrated a highspeed, portable pneumatic system and developed a machine learning model to control harness loading in real time.

Brainstorming

Current exercise harnesses and astronaut exosuits lack the adaptability needed for effective muscle and bone health maintenance in microgravity environments. To address this, our team proposed a novel soft robotic harness system that integrates pneumatic artificial muscles (PAMs) with real-time sensor feedback. The PAMs provide lightweight, natural compliance, and the embedded sensors monitor loading.

I took the lead on the electrical side of the project, focusing on data collection, a pneumatic regulation system, and adaptive control to accomodate user variation and actuator hysteresis. I also collaborated closely with the mechanical team to ensure seamless integration of sensors and actuators.

Interestingly, I joined the Space Wearables team at texlab mid-semeter, when most of the lit review and technical workshops had been completed. This meant learning to build the knit pneumatic actuators and catch up on reading in a fraction of the time while simultaneously managing the hardware and software integration.

Sensor Selection and Calibration

When considering our sensing suite, we knew that loading would vary across the harness, so I proposed a multi-sensor approach that integrated force sensitive resistors (FSRs) with s-type strain-gauge load cells. The FSRs would be embedded in the shoulder straps to capture localized pressure distribution, while the load cells would be mounted inline with the pneumatic actuators to measure the overall tension.

To verify that this approach could reliably judge harness loading, I calibrated the sensors and simulated strap forces to observe potential drift and non-linearity. The calibration process revealed that the load cells provided stable, linear readings, but the FSRs exhibited significant drift.

Additionally, our load cell amplifier is designed to be a USB Native Peripheral Device, which I soon realized would be a bottleneck for our real-time control loop. Because MATLAB operates on a single-thread by default, polling the load cell over the USB bus disrupts the timing of the Arduino's serial communication. So rather than attempting to handle multiple asynchronous hardware protocols, I developed a python script to facilitate hardware communication between MATLAB and the Arduino serial port while using the Phidget22 library to read the load cell voltages.

Pneumatic System

Before I joined the team, previous iterations of the harness focused primarily on sensing and comfort, relying on a wall air compressor for inflation. My role was to transition the project toward "dynamic adjustability" by developing a lightweight and portable air control system. To achieve this, I designed a digital system powered by an Arduino Uno microcontroller. The architecture regulates a micro-vacuum pump and 4/2 solenoid valve using logic-level MOSFET switching circuits. By adjusting the pump's duty cycle and toggling the valve state to switch the actuator port connections, the system achieves precise inflation and active deflation.

During testing, I found that decreasing the sampling interval caused the pump to experience sudden, aggressive jolts. I deduced that rapid pump switching was causing magnetic pushback, which interferred with the pressure sensor power and caused the control loops to overreact. To safely gather training data, I established a baseline sampling rate of 0.2 Hz during initial testing.

Pneumatic Control and ML Model

Standard linear control models like PID fail when managing pneumatic artificial muscles. Because PAMs are constructed from knit sleeves, they exhibit hysteresis: the pressure required to contract the muscle during inflation is completely different from the pressure measured at that exact same length during deflation. If you push the PID gains high enough to overcome this internal friction, the system immediately overshoots and oscillates.

To solve this, I designed an Adaptive Neuro-Fuzzy Inference System (ANFIS) to act as a feedforward component in the control loop. I selected 4 specific inputs for the model to map the system's states:

  • Current Pressure: Tracks our position on the loading/unloading cycle.
  • External Load: Accounts for user compliance and variable body shapes.
  • Force Error: Acts as our primary input signal (Setpoint - Sensor Reading).
  • Change in Force Error: Evaluates how fast we approach the target to prevent overshoot.

Crucially, the sign of this error tells the model which side of the hysteresis loop we are currently on to establish a clean one-to-one mapping for inverse modeling. Put simply, the fuzzification layer utilizes Gaussian membership functions to calculate membership grades for each input, which are then multiplied in the product layer to determine their "firing strength". In the defuzzification stage, a Sugeno rule architecture outputs a weighted average. This output is our control signal, which is fed into the Arduino to drive the pump and valve. The sign will determine the solenoid state, and the magnitude will determine the duty cycle of the pump PWM signal.

Data Collection

Collecting the training data for our model meant implementing a simple PID controller to achieve certain target pressures. The tunning process made it painfully obvious that standard PID control was unfit for our system. The Simulink PID autotuner struggled to converge because of the actuator nonlinearity, so I manually tuned the parameters. I started by increasing the proportional gain until the system could reach the target pressure with minimal overshoot. The integral gains were then slowly increased to achieve a faster response while maintaining accuracy.

Once the actuator could reach the target pressure within an acceptable tolerance, I worked with the team to design a testing rig and subjected the actuators to a 0.05 Hz sinusoidal reference signal varying between 3.447 kPa and 17.24 kPa (0.5 to 2.5 PSI) across three cycles. In each cycle, I applied variable external loads between 0.5 kg and 2 kg. The MATLAB Simulink ANFIS model utilizes a hybrid learning algorithm combining backpropagation gradient descent and least-squares estimation. A major theoretical hurdle was defining the "target force" during training data collection. I evaluated multiple strategies:

  • Force-controlled PID: Allowing a step signal to drive the pump directly via force error.
  • FSR vs. Load Cell Comparison: Using pressure control but evaluating the delta between the FSR and the load cell.
  • Stable Step Response Mapping: Waiting for the pressure to stabilize at various PSI steps and mapping the final achieved force.

Ultimately, I chose to use the FSR readings as the reference force. This meant that the model would be learning to predict the control signal based on the FSR readings rather than the actual load cell force. However, it was the only way to ensure that the training data was consistent enough for the model to learn effectively and capture the transient dynamics.

Final Integration and Results

Finally, I soldered the components together. This was exceptionally difficult, but the result was a compact pneumatic control board. Next, I extracted the optimized premise and consequent parameters from the trained model and converted them into fixed-point lookup matrices directly inside the Arduino firmware.

By pairing the ANFIS feedforward output with a conservative feedback loop, the system effectively mitigated the soft robotic hysteresis. The hybrid control architecture minimized overshoot and successfully adapted the harness across different shoulder configurations.

Reflections

In future iterations, I hope to migrate the ANFIS forward pass to an ESP32 to handle the mathematical processing, and low-level sensor aggregation. My primary focus will also be implementing deadband testing to map the minimum voltage for pump movement, and resolving the pump jolts by isolating electromagnetic feedback and optimizing initial PID gains.

This project put me in the unique position of being the only electrical engineer on a team of mechanical engineers. Furthermore, joining mid semester meant overcoming a steep learning curve. It taught me how to quickly grasp and apply new technical skills. Most importantly, I practiced communicating my ideas effectively to a team with a completely different background. I also learned how to navigate ambiguity and make informed decisions with incomplete information. The experience taught me lessons I’ll carry forward throughout my career.

The overarching framework splits core functionality into reactive behavioral logic (Topics) , synchronous structural setup routines (Services) , and long-running asynchronous tracking tasks (Actions).

Synchronous Initialization via Custom Service Server

Create a synchronous find_wall Service Server (FindWall.srv) that acts as a pre-flight initialization routine. When called, the robot must programmatically find the nearest obstruction, drive straight up to it, flush its alignment parallel to the surface, and return a clean execution state (wallfound = true) before releasing control back to the core tracking loop.

Implementing a multi-phase linear and angular movement routine strictly inside a standard ROS 2 callback block completely froze the node's single-threaded executor stack. Because the service handler took several seconds to complete its spatial searches and wall alignments, it starved the incoming /scan subscriber thread of execution time. Without sensor updates flowing, the node could not read changing laser arrays, causing the robot to spin indefinitely. I chose to utilize a MultiThreadedExecutor and bind the service server callback to an isolated ReentrantCallbackGroup. This architecture separated the long-running service execution pipeline from the sensor ingestion pipeline. The service client could block and command velocities while the secondary callback thread continuously refreshed the internal variables with high-frequency LiDAR scans.

Asynchronous State Estimation via Custom Action Infrastructure

Implement a long-running, non-blocking Action Server (OdomRecord.action) named record_odom. The action client must invoke this server to continuously track, compute, and buffer the robot's spatial odometry frames every second, outputting live mileage feedback and terminating with a definitive vector list of points upon completing a full navigation circuit.

Managing the life cycle of an action client concurrently alongside an operational wall-following timer loop introduced complex thread-synchronization problems. The main challenge was maintaining a completely unblocked, highly deterministic 10Hz operational wall timer loop while simultaneously processing non-blocking asynchronous future objects (async_send_goal) returned from the action client initialization. If the action feedback callbacks or result states were handled on the wrong execution timeline, it caused sudden movements or prematurely issued stop commands (stop_msg) before the robot completed its lap. I addressed this by structuring an event-driven state machine using std::bind to hook isolated callback routines for goal responses, live feedback loops, and final wrapped results. When the action server finally reports a SUCCEEDED status code indicating circuit completion, the result thread intercepts execution, flips the operational flag to false, overrides the timer velocities, and publishes a zero-velocity vector.

The believe the skills I mastered here will serve me well in future work with autonomous mobile robots (AMRs), self-driving vehicles, and complex factory automation cells.

Pipelined Fixed-Point FIR
Filter with UVM Verification

My growing fascination with AI hardware led me to ask: what does that actually look like at the register-transfer level? This project is my answer. I designed a two-stage pipelined MAC engine and FIR filter neuron from scratch in SystemVerilog, deployed it on a Tang Nano 9K FPGA, and built a complete UVM verification environment around it. This is the same methodology used at semiconductor companies like NVIDIA to tape out billion-transistor chips. The final testbench achieved 419 scoreboard passes with zero mismatches and 100% functional coverage closure across all coverpoints, with zero UVM errors or fatals.

2026
Verilog · SystemVerilog · UVM

RTL Design

The design consists of three modules:

  • mac.v — A 2-stage pipelined Multiply-Accumulate unit. Stage 1 registers the product (Q8.8 format). Stage 2 accumulates truncated products and saturates to Q4.4.
  • neuron.sv — A 4-state FSM (IDLE → FETCH → COMPUTE → OUTPUT) that orchestrates the MAC unit, feeds it one (data, weight) pair per clock, and applies a ReLU activation on the result.
  • audio_filter_engine.sv — A stream-interface wrapper with a 3-state FSM (ENG_IDLE → ENG_COMPUTE → ENG_STALL) that implements a valid/ready handshake on both input and output ports, manages the x[n-1] delay line, and stalls the pipeline when the downstream consumer applies backpressure.

  • Count 0: Present (data[0], weight[0]) → Stage 1 registers the product on the next clock.
  • Count 1: Stage 1 product enters Stage 2 accumulator. Present (data[1], weight[1]).
  • Count 2: Stage 1 product enters Stage 2. Present (data[2], weight[2] = bias).
  • Count 3: Bias product enters Stage 2. No new data presented.
  • Count 4: Bias product finishes accumulating. All three terms are now in wide_accum.

The design is a 2-tap FIR filter operating on a signed 8-bit Q4.4 fixed-point stream — a stand-in for either an audio signal or a low-resolution sensor feed. I chose fixed-point because floating point is expensive in area and timing on real silicon, and reasoning carefully about a fixed binary point forces you to confront overflow and precision loss directly instead of letting the format hide it from you. Floating-point arithmetic requires dedicated FPUs that consume significant FPGA fabric and power. For a fixed filter with known weight ranges, Q4.4 fixed-point is sufficient: 4 integer bits and 4 fractional bits, packed into a signed 8-bit value. This gives a real-number range of −8.0 to +7.9375 with a resolution of 0.0625. Every weight and sample in the system uses this format, keeping all arithmetic in simple integer hardware.

The core mac unit is two pipeline stages: a full 16-bit signed multiply register, followed by a 24-bit guard-bit accumulator register. Because the multiplier and accumulator are separate pipeline stages, there's a real hazard to manage — a sample fed into the MAC on cycle N doesn't land in the accumulator until cycle N+2. The neuron FSM that sequences three MAC operations (two taps plus bias) has to explicitly wait for that two-stage latency to drain before it's safe to sample the result — that's the purpose of the otherwise-odd-looking count == 4 condition rather than count == 3. Getting that wrong doesn't throw an error; it silently reads a partially-accumulated value, which is the kind of bug that only shows up as a scoreboard mismatch three verification runs later. The MAC unit (mac.v) implements the core computation across two pipeline stages. Stage 1 registers the full 16-bit product of two Q4.4 inputs (8×8 = 16 bits, Q8.8 format). Stage 2 sign-extends the truncated Q4.4 product into a 24-bit accumulator and adds it to the running sum. The first critical bug I discovered and fixed was that the original 8-bit accumulator silently wrapped around on overflow — a +127 result would reappear as −127. The fix was widening the accumulator to 24 bits, which comfortably holds three Q4.4 products without any loss, then clamping to the signed 8-bit range [−128, +127] combinationally at the output. This converts destructive wraparound into correct saturating arithmetic, which is visible in the waveform: wide_accum climbs above 127 while accum stays pinned at +127.

The subtler design decision was in the top-level audio_filter_engine: the neuron's start signal fires using the current (not-yet-updated) sample registers, but because the neuron itself doesn't latch its inputs until its own STATE_FETCH cycle — one cycle later — it ends up sampling the freshly-updated registers instead. It looks like a bug on first read. It isn't. It's a one-cycle alignment trick that saves a pipeline stage, and it's exactly the kind of thing I wanted my testbench to be rigorous enough to either validate or catch.

Overflow protection came from two layers: saturating arithmetic clamps the accumulator to [-128, 127] before it ever leaves the MAC, and a ReLU activation clips negative results to zero at the output. Both are standard in accelerator datapaths for the same reason — silently wrapping around on overflow is far more dangerous than clipping, because a wrapped value looks like valid data instead of an error.

Building a UART Reciever

To send inputs ($x_0, x_1$) from my PC to your Tang Nano 9K board over a USB cable, you need a UART Receiver. Onboard Crystal Clock ($f_{clk}$): 27 MHz (default on Tang Nano 9K pin 52). Target Baud Rate: 115,200 baud.Clocks per Bit ($N$): $\frac{27,000,000}{115,200} \approx \mathbf{234 \text{ clock cycles per bit}}$. I built a 8N1 (8 data bitd, no parity, 1 stop bit) UART reciever.

UVM Testbench

Rather than eyeballing waveforms, I built a full UVM environment: a randomized transaction class, a constrained-random sequence layer, an active driver/monitor agent pair, an independent reference-model scoreboard, and a functional coverage collector — all wired together through analysis ports rather than direct references, so each component stays decoupled and reusable.

The scoreboard reimplements the exact same fixed-point transfer function as the RTL — y = ReLU(sat((24·x[n] − 16·x[n−1] + 256) >>> 4)) — independently in the testbench, then compares it sample-for-sample against what the monitor observed on the DUT's output. The two independent implementations of the same math have to agree under every stimulus pattern I could throw at them.

For stimulus, I moved past pure randomization early. Standard constrained-random testing is good at finding unexpected bugs, but it's bad at hitting known boundary conditions like max positive/negative saturation, exact zero crossings, heavy backpressure. So I built a family of directed-but-randomized sequences: one purely random baseline (250 samples), an impulse-response sequence to check settling behavior, dedicated saturation-boundary sequences that force the exact extremes, and a heavy-backpressure sequence that stalls the consumer 70% of the time. A master sequence composes all of them into one coherent regression.

Coverage-Driven Closure: Finding the Gaps Numbers Don't Show You

The most instructive part of this project was discovering that a passing testbench and a complete testbench are two different things. Early on, my covergroup had bins for maximum-saturation test modes that no sequence ever actually drove, meaning the coverage tool was permanently reporting a hole that no amount of extra simulation time would ever close. The fix was to write a sequence that explicitly targets those modes.

The harder gap was in the input/output cross-coverage. Because the current-sample tap weight (24) dominates the previous-sample weight (−16) in this filter, several (x[n], y[n]) combinations are mathematically impossible regardless of what x[n−1] was — for example, a maximum-positive input can never produce a near-zero output, no matter how negative the previous sample gets. I worked the transfer function backward to identify exactly which combinations were true hardware constraints rather than test gaps, and marked those as illegal_bins instead of chasing an unreachable 100%. For the combinations that were reachable but only in a narrow input window, I solved the equation directly for the exact x[n−1] needed to hit each one and drove those pairs deterministically — turning "hope random testing gets lucky" into "guaranteed to hit every reachable case exactly once."

I also treated reset as a first-class corner case rather than a one-time setup step. A background reset-injector component fires resets mid-test at randomized intervals — including deliberate back-to-back resets with zero settle time — while the monitor and scoreboard both subscribe to those reset events to resynchronize their own internal state. Without that resynchronization, a reset genuinely would produce a false failure, because the reference model's history and the DUT's history would fall out of sync the instant reset released.

Neuron FSM: Sequencing a 2-Stage Pipeline

The neuron module (neuron.sv) sequences the MAC unit through a four-state FSM: STATE_IDLE, STATE_FETCH, STATE_COMPUTE, and STATE_OUTPUT. The key design challenge was getting the pipeline timing exactly right — a mistake I had to carefully work through.

Because the MAC has two pipeline stages (multiply then accumulate), feeding a new input on cycle N means the accumulated result isn't ready until cycle N+2. For three MAC operations (x[n]×W0, x[n-1]×W1, and the bias term), the pipeline must be fed on cycles 0, 1, and 2, then held idle for two additional flush cycles so the last bias product propagates fully through both stages. The FSM therefore waits until count == 4 before transitioning to STATE_OUTPUT, giving the neuron a total latency of 7 clock cycles from input to result. This is clearly visible in the waveform: current_state stays in STATE_COMPUTE (10) while wide_accum increments through three intermediate values before out_valid rises and out_data presents the final filtered sample.

The ReLU activation is then applied purely combinationally: assign out = mac_accum[7] ? 8'sd0 : mac_accum. Checking bit 7 (the sign bit) of the saturated output and clamping negative values to zero maps directly to the hardware: no comparator, no subtractor — just a single conditional wire.

Stream Interface: Valid/Ready Handshake and Backpressure

The audio_filter_engine module wraps the neuron in an AXI-stream-style valid/ready handshake, making it composable with other streaming IP. The top-level engine FSM has three states: ENG_IDLE (accepting new inputs), ENG_COMPUTE (running the neuron pipeline), and ENG_STALL (holding the output if the downstream consumer isn't ready).

The stall path was one of the more interesting design problems. When neuron_ready fires but out_ready is low, the engine must freeze its output indefinitely — potentially for many cycles — without corrupting the result or missing the next input. ENG_STALL holds out_valid high and drives out_data from a registered holding_out register, blocking in_ready until the downstream consumer acknowledges. The waveform shows this clearly: state remains at ENG_STALL (2'b10) with out_valid held high while out_ready is low, and out_data is frozen at 68 — exactly matching the holding register's value.

The system_top module handles the UART interface: two consecutive bytes received from the host PC are latched as x[n] and x[n-1], then a one-cycle start pulse fires the neuron. The result drives the Tang Nano's six active-low LEDs, making the filter's output visible as a binary pattern on real hardware without any additional display peripherals.

Verification Challenges: Deadlocks, Reset, and Coverage Closure

The most challenging bug in the entire project was a simulation deadlock that manifested as a timeout with no error message. The driver was holding out_ready low (to test backpressure) while simultaneously waiting for in_ready to go high. But in_ready can only assert when the engine is in ENG_IDLE — and ENG_IDLE is only reachable from ENG_STALL when out_ready is 1. Each side was waiting for the other in a classical deadlock. The fix was splitting the driver into two phases: Phase 1 completes the input handshake with out_ready held high (ensuring the engine can return to idle), then Phase 2 applies a timed stall after the input has been accepted. This correctly exercises the ENG_STALL path without ever creating a circular wait.

Mid-test reset injection was another non-trivial challenge. When reset fires during a computation, the neuron pipeline is wiped, but the testbench's monitor had already enqueued that sample in its input FIFO expecting an output that would never arrive. The fix was a dedicated watch_reset thread in the monitor that fires on negedge rst_n, immediately drains the in-flight FIFO, and broadcasts a reset event through a separate analysis port — causing both the scoreboard's delay line (x_prev) and the coverage counter to resynchronize. The testbench successfully handled 5 mid-test resets including back-to-back consecutive pulses, with zero spurious mismatches.

Reaching 100% coverage required one additional directed sequence targeting the high_positive output bin (values 64–126). With random inputs, most outputs either saturate to +127 or clip to 0 via ReLU, statistically skipping the middle range. I calculated mathematically that x[n]=50 with x[n-1]=0 produces y = (1200 + 0 + 256) >> 4 = 91, which falls squarely in [64:126], and designed a targeted sequence that alternates x=50 and x=0 to hit that bin deterministically on every other output.

Protocol Checking with SystemVerilog Assertions

Functional coverage tells you what scenarios were tested; assertions tell you whether the DUT behaved correctly in every cycle. I added four SVA properties directly inside the interface, where they have direct access to all signals and fire automatically without any testbench code needing to call them.

The most important property, p_in_valid_stable, uses the |=> (implies-next-cycle) operator to enforce the AXI-stream rule: if in_valid is high and in_ready is low, then in_valid must still be high on the next clock edge. Dropping it early would cause the DUT to silently miss the transaction. Similarly, p_out_valid_stable and p_out_data_stable guard the stall path: once out_valid asserts, it cannot drop and out_data cannot change until the downstream consumer acknowledges with out_ready. A fourth property, p_out_valid_after_reset, fires on $rose(rst_n) to verify that the pipeline is always empty one cycle after reset — catching any state register that fails to initialize correctly.

All four assertions passed with zero violations across all 419 test transactions and 5 mid-test resets, confirming that the valid/ready protocol was never broken by either the DUT's FSM or the testbench driver throughout the entire simulation.

Results: Full Verification Closure

The final simulation run across the complete master test sequence — 250 random samples, an impulse response, 30 saturation stress samples, 16 directed boundary samples, 20 mid-range output samples, and 40 backpressure stall samples, interrupted by 5 randomized mid-test resets — produced:

  • 419 / 419 SCOREBOARD_PASS — zero mismatches between DUT output and the golden reference model across all inputs including math corner cases, post-reset samples, and stalled transfers.
  • 100% functional coverage — all coverpoints, crosses, and the reset covergroup closed completely. cp_sample_in, cp_sample_out, cp_mode, cp_backpressure, cx_mode_backpressure, cx_input_output, and reset_cg all at 100%.
  • 0 UVM_ERROR, 0 UVM_FATAL — no assertion violations, no scoreboard failures, no monitor underflows.
  • 5 mid-test resets handled cleanly — including back-to-back consecutive pulses — with the scoreboard resynchronizing correctly on every event.

The ReLU canary bin in cp_sample_out — the unexpected_neg bin covering outputs in [−128:−1] — recorded zero hits throughout all 419 transactions, confirming that the ReLU activation correctly eliminates all negative outputs with no exceptions.

Results & Learnings

Debugging this environment mostly meant reading waveforms alongside the UVM log in parallel rather than trusting either one alone. The reset waveform is a good example: watching rst_n drop and recover mid-stream while wide_accum snaps back to zero, and confirming the very next scoreboard comparison after release still matches, was the actual proof that the resynchronization logic worked — the coverage number alone wouldn't have told me that.

The final regression closed clean: 419 of 419 sample comparisons matched with zero mismatches across 5 injected reset events, 100% functional coverage across all three covergroups (output values, test intent, and reset events), and a UVM report of 910 informational messages with zero warnings, errors, or fatals.

The current design is a single, time-multiplexed MAC reused across taps — area-efficient, but it stalls until each sample fully drains before accepting the next one. The natural next step, and the one that ties most directly back to what pulled me into this project, is replacing that with an array of parallel MAC units fed by a tapped delay line, so a new sample can enter the pipeline every cycle instead of waiting — a small, honest step toward the systolic structures that make real AI accelerators fast. That version introduces a genuinely new hazard class worth verifying: samples overlapping in-flight through the pipeline, rather than the single-sample-at-a-time hazards this version already handles.

More broadly, this project reinforced something I don't think you get from RTL alone: a design that "looks correct" and a design that's been proven correct against every boundary condition, including the ones a human wouldn't think to check by hand, are very different claims. Working through the math to find provably unreachable coverage bins, rather than assuming they were test gaps, was the part of this project that felt closest to how verification actually works at the scale these chips are built.
